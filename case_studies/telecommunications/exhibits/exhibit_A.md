---

### Summary

Last quarter's retention spend reached **$4.2 million** — a 34% increase over the prior quarter — while the churn rate rose two basis points to its highest level since Meridian's founding. This memo summarizes the disconnect between retention investment and subscriber outcomes and recommends a fundamental change in approach.

---

### 1. The Numbers

| Metric | Q3 | Q4 | Change |
|--------|-----|-----|--------|
| Retention spend | $3.13M | $4.20M | +34.2% |
| Subscribers receiving retention offers | 41,200 | 58,900 | +42.9% |
| Net subscriber gain/(loss) | +1,100 | (3,200) | — |
| Churn rate (annualized) | 18.4% | 18.6% | +20 bps |
| Avg. retention credit per offer | $53.20 | $71.30 | +34.0% |

**Bottom line:** We spent 34% more on retention, offered credits to 43% more subscribers, and lost 3,200 net subscribers. The program is not working.

---

### 2. Where the Money Went

A post-hoc analysis of Q4 retention offers reveals a troubling pattern:

| Offer Recipient Profile | % of Offers | Est. % Who Were Genuinely At Risk |
|------------------------|------------|----------------------------------|
| Subscriber called to cancel | 38% | ~85% |
| Subscriber flagged by usage decline | 22% | ~60% |
| Subscriber requested by account manager | 12% | Unknown |
| Subscriber triggered by contract expiry | 28% | ~35% |

Roughly **40% of retention credits** went to subscribers who showed **zero churn signals** in the prior twelve months. These subscribers called in after hearing — from friends, social media, or competitor advertising — that threatening to cancel would trigger a discount offer. We are not retaining at-risk customers. We are subsidizing loyal ones.

I have started calling this phenomenon **"the loyalty tax"** — the hidden cost of a reactive retention program that rewards the loudest voices rather than the most vulnerable subscribers.

---

### 3. The Discount Spiral

The average retention credit has climbed from $38 two quarters ago to $71 last quarter. Three factors are driving this:

1. **Competitive escalation.** National carriers have been offering switcher credits of $200–$400. Our front-line agents feel pressure to match.

2. **Repeat claimants.** We identified approximately 8,400 subscribers who received retention credits in *both* Q3 and Q4. These subscribers have learned the system. The average credit for repeat claimants was $94 — 32% higher than for first-time recipients.

3. **No risk stratification.** Our agents have no tool to distinguish a genuinely at-risk subscriber from an opportunistic caller. Every cancellation threat is treated identically.

At the current trajectory, retention spend will exceed **$17 million annualized** — representing nearly 7% of subscriber revenue. This is unsustainable.

---

### 4. What I'm Asking For

I am requesting that Priya Sharma's team develop a **predictive churn model** that can be embedded into our CRM system to provide agents with real-time risk scores during retention calls. Specifically:

- **Risk stratification at the subscriber level.** Before an agent offers a credit, they should know whether this subscriber is genuinely at risk or gaming the system.

- **Proactive outreach.** Instead of waiting for cancellation calls, identify at-risk subscribers and intervene *before* they decide to leave — with targeted offers, service improvements, or plan reviews.

- **Budget discipline.** The retention budget should be allocated by risk tier, not distributed reactively. High-risk subscribers warrant investment. Low-risk subscribers who call to negotiate should receive empathy, not cash.

I am also asking Renata's team to model the financial impact of redirecting retention spend from reactive credits to proactive, model-driven interventions. If we can cut wasted retention spend by 30–40%, the savings fund the analytics initiative several times over.

---

### 5. A Word of Caution

Priya has flagged a concern that I want to put on the record early. Preliminary analysis suggests that two strong churn predictors — contract type and payment method — correlate significantly with subscriber age and digital engagement. If we build a retention model on these features and act on it aggressively, we risk the appearance (or the reality) of age-based profiling.

The AARP complaint against TelcoNorth is public. Their model used nearly identical features to segment subscribers for differential pricing. We need to get this right — both analytically and ethically — before we deploy anything.

I've asked Priya to include an ethical review in the project scope and to present options to the Customer Committee.
